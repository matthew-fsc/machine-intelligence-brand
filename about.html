A publication about correspondences, and their limits

peplomer reads the brain and the model side by side. Not to claim they are the same, but to say, carefully, where they rhyme and where the rhyme runs out.

The subject is the seam between behavioral science, neuroscience, and machine learning, prompt engineering, and agentic architecture. The reader is a working practitioner who builds with language models and agents, or a researcher who knows the brain side and wants the machine mapping done without hand-waving. The essays are long, technical, and free of hype, and every one of them is built to survive its own most sceptical section.

The device

One structural choice runs through everything: a fixed accent legend. Each colour has a single meaning and holds it site-wide, so the palette carries information rather than decoration.

AccentMeaningWhere it appears
blue the biological side: neuroscience, behavior, wetware essays whose subject is a brain or behavioral finding
red the computational side: models, training, architecture essays whose subject is a machine mechanism
yellow the bridge: a claimed correspondence between the two only where a mapping is being asserted, never as filler

Yellow is rationed on purpose. If it appeared everywhere, it would mean nothing. It marks the moment a claim of correspondence is actually being made, in a bridge line, a table rating, or the one essay whose whole subject is a mapping strong enough to earn the colour.

The fidelity rating

Every analogy on this site carries one of three words, and the word is a promise about how far you can push the comparison.

  1. Mechanism. The same causal process, not only the same shape. Rare, and reserved for cases like the dopaminergic prediction error matching the temporal-difference update in single neurons.
  2. Analogy. A structural match that predicts and transfers intuition, but runs through different machinery. Most useful correspondences live here.
  3. Metaphor. A figure of speech that is load-bearing for intuition only. Productive for generating hypotheses, dangerous for explaining results.

Where the mapping breaks

Every essay has a required section with that title, styled as its own block. Nothing here claims a correspondence without also stating its limit. The section is not a disclaimer bolted on at the end; it is the test the rest of the essay has to pass. A claim that cannot survive its own limits does not belong in the piece.

The name

The mark is a virus cell: a ringed core with evenly spaced spikes, each a stem ending in a knob, the peplomers of a coronavirus. The name follows the mark, and it is a working title. Three candidates fit the mark and the seam concept.

candidate wordmarks
  1. peplomer: the spike protein itself, the mark made literal; obscure, technical, journal-like. In use now.
  2. capsid: the protein shell of a virus; the container that holds the essays.
  3. corpus: a body of work, and the corpus callosum that bridges two hemispheres.

Swapping the name is a one-line change: replace the wordmark text in each page header and footer, and the favicon needs no edit since the mark stands on its own.

How it is built

A static site: plain HTML, one stylesheet, a small file of vanilla JavaScript, and no build step. Every diagram is inline, hand-written SVG that carries information rather than decoration. Fonts are Fraunces for display, Newsreader for reading, and IBM Plex Mono for data and labels. It opens by double-clicking a file, respects reduced-motion preferences, and keeps its focus rings visible. There are no trackers and no third-party dependencies beyond the fonts.
