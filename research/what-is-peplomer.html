What peplomer is

A reading guide to a publication that puts the brain and the machine side by side, and refuses to pretend they are the same.

reading time · 4 min depth · primer last updated · 2026-08-13
the one-sentence version

peplomer reads the brain and the machine side by side, asks where they genuinely line up, and rates how much you are allowed to trust each comparison.

There is a lot of loose talk about machines that think like brains. Some of it is careful, most of it is not, and the difference matters if you build with these systems for a living. peplomer exists to do the careful version in public: take a concept from neuroscience or behavioral science, set it next to its counterpart in machine learning, and work out, slowly, how deep the resemblance really goes.

The house style is skeptical on purpose. A resemblance that survives hard questions is worth building on. One that does not should be named as a figure of speech before it quietly becomes load-bearing in someone's mental model. So every comparison here comes with two things attached: a rating of how strong it is, and a section that states, plainly, where it falls apart.

What you will find here

Two devices run through everything. The first is the correspondence table: a neuroscience concept in one column, the machine-learning concept in the next, and a one-word rating in the third. The second is a required section titled where the mapping breaks. Nothing here claims a correspondence without also stating its limit, because the limit is usually where the useful thinking is.

Writing comes at more than one depth. Some pieces are primers like this one, meant to hand you a mental model in a few minutes with no mathematics. Others are longer essays that do the full work, with figures, references, and the argument laid out end to end. Each piece is tagged so you can pick the level you want.

Who it is for

Two readers, mostly. The engineer who builds with language models and agents and wants the brain analogies done rigorously rather than decoratively. And the researcher who knows the biology and wants the machine-learning mapping made honest. You do not need both backgrounds. The primers assume neither; the essays assume patience.

The name and the mark

The mark is a virus: a ringed core with evenly spaced spikes, each a stem ending in a knob, the peplomers that stud a coronavirus. peplomer is the name of that spike, and it is a working title, swappable in one line. The mark is not decoration. Its spikes carry the site's colours: the biological side on the left, the computational side on the right, and the single bridge spike at the top, which is the whole idea in one glyph.
