Framing, anchoring, and the behavioral economics of the prompt

Prompt sensitivity looks like framing, position bias looks like the serial-position curve, and leading eval prompts look like demand characteristics. The resemblance is real and the mechanisms are not shared.

abstract

Human judgement bends to how a question is framed, to an irrelevant anchor, to what came just before, and to what the subject thinks the experimenter wants. Language-model outputs bend to prompt phrasing, to a fact's position in a long context, to the order of few-shot examples, and to leading questions in evaluations. The surface similarity is striking. The underlying causes are almost certainly unrelated, which is exactly why the analogy is productive for generating hypotheses and dangerous for explaining results. The central analogy is metaphor, and that rating is the point.

01The human effects

Behavioural economics is, in large part, a catalogue of the ways context moves a judgement that logically should not move. AnchoringThe tendency for an initial number, even an irrelevant one, to pull subsequent numerical estimates toward it.: Tversky and Kahneman spun a wheel of fortune, and subjects' estimates of an unrelated quantity were pulled toward whatever number the wheel had landed on 1. FramingThe finding that logically equivalent descriptions of the same choice, such as survival versus mortality rates, produce systematically different preferences.: the same policy described in terms of lives saved versus lives lost flips the majority preference, though the outcomes are identical 2. Priming: exposure to one stimulus changes the response to a later one, as when reading a word speeds recognition of a related word 3. Order and recency effects shuffle judgements by sequence alone.

These are robust, replicated, and, for the most part, mechanistically mysterious in their own right. They are usually explained by appeal to heuristics, limited attention, and the architecture of human memory, but the explanations are themselves debated. What matters here is that the effects are real and that they share a form: a variable that a rational agent should ignore reliably shifts the response.

02The prompt versions

Every one of those effects has a machine-shaped twin. Prompt sensitivity is the headline: trivial changes in wording, formatting, or separators move a model's output, sometimes by margins large enough to swamp the difference between models. Sclar and colleagues quantified how much accuracy swings under formatting choices that should be semantically inert, spacing, casing, delimiter style, and found the spread alarming 4. That is framing, transposed: logically equivalent presentations, different answers.

The anchoring twin is just as easy to elicit. Put a number in the prompt, ask for an unrelated estimate, and the output leans toward the number. Ask a leading question and the model tends to confirm its presupposition. To a behavioural scientist these look like old friends, and that recognition is genuinely useful: the human literature is a ready-made list of manipulations to try, a source of hypotheses about where a model will prove brittle. The catalogue transfers even if the mechanism does not.

The human catalogue transfers as a list of things to try, not as an explanation of why they work.

03Position and order

Two effects are precise enough to measure cleanly and worth separating from the rest. Position biasThe tendency of a model to use information differently depending on where it sits in the context, often neglecting the middle of a long input. in long contexts: Liu and colleagues showed that models use information best when it appears at the beginning or end of the context and worst when it is buried in the middle, the now-familiar lost-in-the-middle curve 5. That looks like primacy and recency, the serial-position curve from human memory, and the resemblance is close enough to be eerie.

Few-shot ordering is the other. The sequence in which examples are presented changes a model's predictions, sometimes dramatically, and Lu and colleagues showed that a good ordering and a bad ordering of the same examples can span most of the achievable accuracy range 6. Zhao and colleagues traced part of this to a measurable bias toward certain answers that can be estimated and subtracted, calibrating the model before use 7. The human twin is the order effect; the difference is that the model's version can be characterised numerically and partly corrected, which the human version resists.

04Demand characteristics in evals

There is one more human effect that maps too well onto how we test models. Demand characteristicsCues in an experiment that convey what response is expected, leading subjects to supply it regardless of their true state. are the cues in an experiment that tell a subject what the experimenter wants, so the subject obliges, and Orne showed how thoroughly they can contaminate a result 8. An evaluation prompt that signals the desired answer, through phrasing, through the framing of the options, through what a helpful assistant is supposed to say, invites exactly this. A model trained to be agreeable is unusually susceptible to the machine version of demand characteristics, and it is a live confound in any eval whose prompt leaks its own expectations.

where the mapping breaks

Same surface, unrelated causes

The surface similarity is striking, and the underlying causes are almost certainly unrelated. Human framing effects arise from a specific cognitive architecture: loss aversion, reference-dependent value, limited attention, the way affect colours a description before deliberation begins. Model framing effects arise from token-level statistics: a differently worded prompt is a different point in input space, routed through attention to a different region of the learned function, with no reference point, no loss aversion, and no affect anywhere in the mechanism. The two systems land on the same behaviour from entirely different places.

This is why the fidelity is metaphor and why that is not a demotion. The analogy is dangerous exactly when it is used to explain: to say a model "anchors because it is biased like people are" smuggles in a mechanism, reference-dependent judgement, that the model does not have, and it will lead you to predict the wrong interventions. Debiasing that works on humans, warnings, incentives, consider-the-opposite, has no reason to work on a model, and calibration methods that work on a model have no bearing on people.

But the analogy is productive exactly when it is used to generate. The human literature is a century-old, well-organised map of the places where context illegitimately moves a judgement. Read as a source of hypotheses, where to probe, what manipulation to try next, which brittleness to expect, it is one of the most useful inputs a model evaluator has. The discipline is to let it suggest experiments and never let it stand in for their explanation.

06Implications for prompt and agent design

  • Mine the bias catalogue for tests, not for theories. Use anchoring, framing, order, and demand effects as a checklist of manipulations to run against your prompts and evals. Do not use them to explain why a model failed.
  • Measure prompt sensitivity as a first-class metric. Report accuracy across paraphrases and formats, not a single prompt. A result that survives only one phrasing is a result about the phrasing.
  • Place critical facts at the ends, and calibrate order. Given position bias, put must-use information early or late, and estimate and subtract few-shot ordering bias rather than hoping a lucky order holds.
  • Audit eval prompts for leaked expectations. If the prompt signals the desired answer, an agreeable model will supply it. Strip demand characteristics before you trust the score.

07Open questions

  • The lost-in-the-middle curve and the human serial-position curve share a shape. Is that pure coincidence, an artefact of training data written by position-biased humans, or a deeper property of sequence processing?
  • Model biases can often be estimated and subtracted. Is there a general calibration that removes a whole class of prompt-induced bias, or must each be handled on its own?
  • If human and model context effects are mechanistically unrelated, is there any regime where the human debiasing toolkit does transfer, and how would we recognise it?
