The predicting brain, in plain terms

Your brain is not waiting for the world to tell it what is out there. It is guessing, constantly, and paying attention mostly to what it got wrong.

reading time · 5 min depth · primer last updated · 2026-08-13

Here is a picture of the brain that has quietly taken over a lot of neuroscience. Your brain is not sitting back and waiting for the world to report in. It is guessing what is about to happen, all the time, at every level, and then checking the guess against what actually arrives. Most of what you experience is the guess. What reaches awareness as news is mostly the part the brain got wrong.

the key idea

The brain runs on prediction. It generates an expectation, compares it to the incoming signal, and passes along mainly the difference, the part it did not see coming.

The brain guesses first

Think about catching a ball, or finishing someone's ______. You did not wait for all the information. Your brain projected ahead and prepared a response to a world it had not fully seen yet. This is not a party trick; it appears to be the ordinary mode of operation. Perception, on this view, is less like a camera and more like a running hypothesis that sensory input keeps nudging.

Surprise is the signal

If the brain already predicted something correctly, that prediction carries little new information, so little of it travels onward. What travels is the mismatch, the prediction error. Learning is the brain adjusting its internal model so that next time the error is smaller. A world that matched your expectations perfectly would, in a sense, be invisible. You notice what surprises you.

Attention turns up the volume

Not every surprise deserves a response. A flicker in a noisy channel might be nothing; the same flicker in a channel you trust might be important. The brain weights its prediction errors by how reliable it thinks the source is, and that weighting is a good part of what we call attention. To attend to something is to let its errors count more toward changing your mind.

Why this matters for machines

The reason this primer sits on a machine-learning publication is that the machines are, in one narrow sense, doing something with the same shape. A language model is trained by predicting what comes next and adjusting itself when it is wrong. That is a genuine resemblance, and it is also where people overreach. The brain and the model both minimise a prediction error, but for different reasons and under different rules, and the full essay is about exactly how far that likeness holds.
