Predictive processing and the prediction objective

The brain spends its life guessing the next signal and correcting the guess. So does a language model. The shared verb is real. The reasons behind it are not the same.

abstract

Predictive processing casts the brain as a hierarchy that continuously predicts its own sensory input and passes only the error upward. Modern sequence models are trained by predicting the next token and propagating a loss gradient. Both are prediction-error minimisers, and the framing transfers useful intuitions in both directions. But the brain's objective is derived from a normative principle, free-energy minimisation, while the model's objective is an engineering choice, cross-entropy, selected because it is differentiable and cheap. The essay holds the analogy at analogy and shows the seams.

01The shared objective

Start with the least controversial version of the claim. A cortical column receiving input does not passively register it. It carries a generative modelAn internal model that can produce, or predict, the sensory data it expects, rather than only classifying data after the fact. of what should be arriving, subtracts that prediction from what actually arrives, and forwards the residual. This is the core of predictive codingA theory in which each level of a sensory hierarchy predicts the activity of the level below and transmits only the prediction error upward. as Rao and Ballard framed it for visual cortex 1, later generalised by Friston into the free-energy account of the whole brain 2.

A transformer trained on text does something with a recognisable shape. At each position it produces a distribution over the next token, compares it against the token that actually occurs, and adjusts its parameters to make the observed token more likely next time. The training signal is the cross-entropy between prediction and outcome. Reduce it, token after token, corpus after corpus, and the model comes to encode a great deal about the structure that generated the text.

The verbs line up. Both systems predict, both measure a gap, both change internal state to shrink the gap. Andy Clark's synthesis made this the organising metaphor of a decade of cognitive science 3, and it is why the arrival of large language models felt, to people who had read that literature, less like a break than a confirmation. The prediction objective was already the leading candidate for what cortex computes.

02Precision and attention

The more interesting correspondence is not the loss but the gating on top of it. In predictive processing, not all prediction errors are treated as equally informative. Each error is weighted by its precisionThe expected reliability, or inverse variance, of a signal. High-precision errors are trusted and drive learning; low-precision errors are discounted as noise., the brain's estimate of how reliable that signal is. A high-precision error in a trusted channel should revise beliefs; a low-precision error in a noisy channel should be discounted. Precision weighting is the theory's account of attention: to attend is to raise the precision on a subset of prediction errors 24.

Self-attention in a transformer is a different mechanism with a similar job description. A query vector scores every key, the scores are normalised, and the resulting weights decide how much each position contributes to the next representation. The network learns which positions deserve weight in which context. Both systems, then, hold a learned policy for allocating a scarce resource, influence over the current estimate, across competing signals.

The rhyme is worth taking seriously because it predicts failures. If attention is precision allocation, then miscalibrated precision should produce hallucination: a prior held with too much confidence overrides sensory error, and the system perceives what it expected rather than what arrived. That is a standard predictive-processing account of hallucination in psychosis 4. A language model that attends too strongly to its own prior context, and too weakly to a retrieved fact, fails in a structurally similar way. The mapping earns its keep here: it tells you where to look.

To attend is to decide which errors are allowed to change your mind.

03World models and the generative stack

Prediction alone is cheap. Prediction that generalises requires a model of the causes behind the data, not just the data's surface statistics. In the brain, the generative model is hierarchical: low levels predict edges and contours, higher levels predict objects and scenes, and the whole stack is trained by the errors that survive each level of prediction. The claim of predictive processing is that perception just is the settling of this hierarchy into the explanation that leaves the least error unexplained.

Sequence models were argued to acquire something similar as a side effect of the prediction objective. Elman showed decades ago that a network trained only to predict the next word in a sentence develops internal structure that tracks grammatical category and number 5. Scaled up, next-token prediction over a large corpus appears to induce representations of syntax, of factual relations, and of at least some causal structure, because those regularities are what make the next token predictable. The explicit version of the idea, a learned latent model of environment dynamics that an agent can roll forward, is the world modelA learned internal simulator of how an environment evolves, which an agent can run forward to plan without acting in the world. line of work 6.

This is the strongest part of the analogy and also the most seductive. It is easy to slide from "the model predicts the next token well" to "the model has a world model like ours." The first is measured. The second is an inference about internal structure that requires its own evidence, and the evidence is mixed: models carry usable structure for some domains and confidently wrong structure for others, with no reliable signal at the surface to tell the two apart.

04Active inference and tool use

The brain does not only update its model to fit the world. It also acts on the world to make the world fit its model. Active inferenceThe idea that action and perception both serve to reduce prediction error: you can change your belief to match the data, or change the data by acting. treats action as another way to reduce prediction error: if you predict your hand is on the cup, you can either revise the prediction or move your hand until the prediction comes true 2. Perception and action become two routes to the same objective.

An agent built on a language model, given tools, has a loop with the same two moves. It can update its estimate of the task state from what it reads, and it can call a tool, run code, query an API, edit a file, to change the environment so that the next observation matches its plan. The correspondence is at the level of the loop structure, and it is genuinely useful: it suggests that an agent's tool calls should be understood as epistemic and pragmatic actions, some taken to gather information that will sharpen the next prediction, some taken to drive the world toward the goal. Designers who separate those two intents write better agents.

where the mapping breaks

The resemblance is partly notational

Free energy is a normative framework. It claims that any system that persists must, on average, minimise the surprise of its sensory states, and it derives a bound, the variational free energy, that the brain is hypothesised to be minimising as an imperative of remaining a self-organising system. Cross-entropy is an engineering choice. It was selected for next-token training because it is the maximum-likelihood loss for a categorical output, it is differentiable, and its gradient is convenient. Nobody argues a transformer will dissolve if its loss rises. It simply trains worse.

Both quantities can be written as a divergence between a predicted and an observed distribution, and that shared algebra is most of what makes the analogy feel tight. But sameness of formula is not sameness of cause. The free-energy account carries commitments, about homeostasis, about the imperative to exist, about action as inference, that cross-entropy training does not have and does not need. When someone says a language model "minimises surprise like the brain," the true content is the divergence formula, and the rest is imported for free.

Two more seams. First, timing: the brain's predictive loop runs continuously and bidirectionally during inference, while a trained model's prediction error is a signal at training time only; at inference it runs a fixed forward pass with no error to propagate. Second, the objective's target: the brain minimises the surprise of a body's sensory states over a lifetime, tied to survival; the model minimises the surprise of a token given a corpus, tied to nothing but the corpus. The optimisation looks alike. What is being optimised for could hardly be more different.

06Implications for prompt and agent design

  • Treat context as precision, not just content. If attention behaves like precision allocation, then the ordering and salience of context set which signals win. Put the fact you need the model to trust where it will be weighted, not merely where it is present.
  • Expect prior-driven error when the prompt is confident and wrong. A strongly framed but incorrect premise is a high-precision prior. The model will perceive the task through it. Lower that precision by explicitly flagging assumptions as provisional.
  • Separate epistemic and pragmatic tool calls. Design agents so that "act to learn" and "act to change the world" are distinct, logged intents. Retrieval and probing reduce uncertainty; writes commit to it. Conflating them hides the moment a plan went wrong.
  • Do not infer a world model from low loss. Good next-token accuracy is evidence of surface predictability, not of a correct internal simulator. Test the model of the world you care about directly.

07Open questions

  • Does anything in a trained transformer play the functional role of precision, a learned, context-dependent estimate of a signal's reliability that is separable from its content?
  • Predictive coding runs error correction at inference time. Would giving models an inference-time error signal, beyond in-context conditioning, close part of the gap, or is that just fine-tuning by another name?
  • If active inference is the right frame for agents, what is the model's equivalent of a body: the fixed cost structure that would make some prediction errors matter for its persistence rather than only for a score?
