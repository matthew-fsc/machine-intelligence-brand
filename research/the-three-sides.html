The three sides: biological, computational, and the bridge

Three colours carry the whole argument. Blue is a claim about brains, red is a claim about machines, and yellow is a claim that the two line up.

reading time · 5 min depth · primer last updated · 2026-08-13

Three colours carry the entire argument of this site, and they are not chosen for decoration. Each one marks a different kind of claim, and keeping them apart is what keeps the thinking honest. Blue is a claim about brains. Red is a claim about machines. Yellow is a claim that the two line up.

the whole system, once

Blue and red describe two systems on their own terms. Yellow is the only colour that asserts a relationship between them, and it is the only colour that ever carries a fidelity rating.

Blue: claims about the brain

Anything painted blue is a statement about biology: how neurons fire, how memory consolidates, how attention is allocated, how a behavioral effect shows up in human subjects. Blue claims answer to neuroscience and psychology. They are true or false about biology, and nothing about a machine changes whether they hold.

Red: claims about the machine

Anything red is a statement about computation: what a loss function does, how attention weights are computed, what happens during training, how an agent uses a tool. Red claims answer to machine learning. They stand or fall on their own, whether or not any brain works the same way.

Yellow: claims that the two correspond

Yellow is the interesting colour and the dangerous one. A yellow claim says a blue thing and a red thing are versions of the same idea. That is a real assertion, and it can be wrong in ways that a blue or a red claim cannot, because it depends on both systems at once and on the relationship between them.

Because yellow claims are the ones that mislead, they are the only ones that get a fidelity rating, one of three words: mechanism, analogy, or metaphor. And they are rationed. If yellow appeared everywhere it would mean nothing; it shows up only where a correspondence is actually being asserted.

Why keep them apart

Most confusion about brains and machines comes from a colour slip: a red fact dressed up as a blue one, or a yellow guess treated as a red result. A model that attends to a token is not thereby paying attention in the human sense; that would be a yellow claim, and it needs a rating before you lean on it. Holding the three colours separate is a small discipline that prevents a large class of mistakes.
